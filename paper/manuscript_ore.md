# Reading the picture or reading the data? An open benchmark of career-map Sankey diagrams for small vision-language models

## Abstract

### Background

Career guidance platforms such as XR-CareerAssist show possible career paths as Sankey diagrams, in which bands of varying width show how many people moved between roles or industries over time. A virtual assistant that explains these maps must answer questions about them correctly. It can read the picture with a vision-language model or read the table of numbers behind it, but the two options have not been compared.

### Methods

This study introduces SankeyQA, an open benchmark of 600 artificial career cohorts drawn as career maps, with 6,560 questions whose answers are computed exactly from the underlying tables. The data are not real. They were generated by a computer to demonstrate and test the technology, and contain no personal data. Five small open models that run on a laptop processor were tested on 875 questions about 80 unseen maps, four reading the picture and one reading the table as text. The study also varied how the maps were drawn and fine-tuned one picture model on 20, 100 or 400 maps chosen at random or for diversity.

### Results

Looking the answer up in the table was always correct. The best picture reader answered 30.1% of questions correctly and the table reader 38.9%, while a guess that never looks at the map reached 40.5%. The picture readers failed on numbers printed in the map and on questions that require following flows. Fine-tuning the picture model raised accuracy from 12.7% to 55.5% with 400 maps. Twenty maps chosen for diversity were no better than twenty random maps.

### Conclusions

Small vision-language models cannot yet be trusted to explain career maps. An assistant should answer from the data that produced the picture and use the picture only to show the answer. SankeyQA, its generator and all results are openly available for testing future models.

## Keywords

Sankey diagram, vision-language model, chart question answering, career guidance, extended reality, benchmark, fine-tuning, synthetic data

## Plain language summary

Some career guidance tools show people where others with a similar background ended up after 2, 5 or 10 years. They use a kind of chart called a Sankey diagram, where coloured bands flow from left to right and thicker bands mean more people. In our virtual reality platform, XR-CareerAssist, a talking avatar explains these charts. We wanted to know whether a small artificial intelligence model can look at such a chart and answer questions about it correctly, or whether the assistant should instead read the numbers that the chart was drawn from. We built 600 artificial career maps with a computer program, so that no real person's data were used. The maps are not real careers, they only serve to test the technology, and wrote thousands of questions with known answers. Small image-reading models answered fewer than one in three questions correctly, less often than a simple guess that ignores the chart. A small text model reading the numbers did not do much better, but a direct look-up in the numbers was always right. Training one image model on example maps helped a lot, but it still made many mistakes. We conclude that assistants should answer from the data and use the chart only to illustrate the answer. All maps, questions, code and results are freely available.

## Introduction

Career guidance tools increasingly show people pictures of possible futures. A common picture is the Sankey diagram, in which coloured bands flow from left to right and the width of each band shows how many people moved from one role, or one industry, to another [@schmidt2008, @riehmann2005]. The XR-CareerAssist platform, developed within the VOXReality programme, places such career maps inside a virtual reality headset [@tantaroudas2026salento, @tantaroudas2026ore, @tantaroudas2026arxiv]. A user answers a short questionnaire about their current role, years of experience, industry, age group and target role. The platform then finds professionals with similar profiles in a database of more than 100,000 anonymised CVs and shows where those people were 2, 5 and 10 years later. A 3D avatar explains the map in the user's language, using speech recognition, machine translation, a conversational agent and speech synthesis [@tantaroudas2026ore]. Related platforms of the same programme use the same chain of speech and language services for inclusive communication and language learning [@tantaroudas2026interact, @tantaroudas2026language].

Sankey diagrams are compact, but they are not easy to read. Readers have to compare the widths of curved bands, which people judge less accurately than positions or lengths [@cleveland1984], and the bands cross each other when many groups exchange members [@zarate2018]. Career guidance theory stresses that people should explore and discuss their possible careers rather than receive a single recommendation [@savickas2012, @leung2022], and earlier career guidance systems already used network pictures to show possible paths [@josegarcia2023] or virtual reality to show workplaces [@garcia2021]. A career map therefore needs a guide who can answer questions such as "where do most managers end up after ten years?" or "is it more common to become a director or a consultant?". Natural-language explanations of charts also make them accessible to people who cannot see them well [@lundgard2022].

During the XR-CareerAssist project the team tried to give the avatar this ability by fine-tuning a vision-language model, a neural network that reads an image and answers questions about it in words, on pictures of career maps [@tantaroudas2026ore]. The model was BLIP [@li2022blip]. Two observations from that work motivate the present study. First, training on 1,280 near-identical diagrams made the model memorise them, whereas 20 diagrams chosen by k-means clustering to differ from each other gave better answers. Second, the conversational agent, which answered in words without looking at the picture, guided users through the maps better than the vision-language model, and the platform therefore relied on the conversational agent [@tantaroudas2026ore]. Neither observation could be measured, because the project had no set of questions with known answers.

This article provides such a set and the measurements. Because a Sankey diagram is drawn from a table of counts, every question about it has an exact answer that can be computed from the table. This makes it possible to build a benchmark of any size without anyone labelling pictures by hand. Benchmarks of this kind exist for bar, line and pie charts [@kahou2018, @kafle2018, @methani2020, @masry2022], but, as far as the authors are aware, not for Sankey diagrams, whose information lies in the widths and connections of bands. The study asks four questions.

- How well do small, openly available vision-language models answer questions about career-map Sankey diagrams, and which kinds of questions do they get right or wrong?
- Do their answers change when the same data are drawn differently, for example with a transparent background or at a lower resolution?
- When a vision-language model is fine-tuned, does a small set of diverse diagrams help more than random diagrams under the same training budget, and how much do more diagrams help?
- Is it better for an assistant to read the picture or to read the table that the picture was drawn from?

The study needs no participants. It uses simulated career cohorts instead of the CVCOSMOS database, so that no personal data are involved and every number behind every diagram is known. All models are small enough to run on an ordinary laptop processor, which suits a headset application whose processing has to be cheap. The contributions are an open benchmark, SankeyQA, with its generator, 6,560 questions and 1,260 images, an evaluation of five models and two simple baselines on 875 test questions, a test of eleven drawing styles and a controlled fine-tuning experiment. The code is on GitHub and the data are archived on Zenodo [@sankeyqadata].

## Methods

### Overview

Figure 1 shows the pipeline. A questionnaire configuration produces a simulated cohort of profiles, the cohort is summarised in a flow table, the flow table is drawn as a career map, and questions are generated from the flow table together with their exact answers. Two kinds of readers then answer the questions. Picture readers receive only the image of the career map, as a vision-language model in the headset would. Table readers receive the flow table, either as text for a language model or as data for a direct look-up. Every reply is compared with the exact answer. All steps are carried out by the open-source Python package `sankeyqa` with fixed random seeds, and the whole study ran on a cloud machine with two processor cores and no graphics card [@sankeyqadata].

### Career maps without personal data

The career maps of XR-CareerAssist are built from the anonymised profiles of the CVCOSMOS database, which cannot be shared [@tantaroudas2026ore]. The benchmark therefore simulates cohorts. A configuration has the same fields as the platform's questionnaire, namely the kind of map (roles or industries), the starting role, years of experience (2 to 25), industry (12 options), age group (21–30 to 51–60), target role and the number of matched profiles (60 to 1,200). Roles form a ladder of six levels, from employee to executive, plus two side roles, consultant and founder. For each configuration a small random model moves every simulated person between roles or industries at 2, 5 and 10 years. People with less experience and in younger age groups move more often, upward moves are pulled towards the target role, and moves between related industries are more likely than moves between unrelated ones. As in the real platform, each stage shows only the largest groups (four to seven), and the remaining people are merged into a group called Other.

The data are therefore artificial and not real. They were generated only to demonstrate and test the technology, and no conclusion about real careers, roles or industries should be drawn from them. The simulation is not meant to describe real careers. It produces diagrams with the same structure as those of the platform, a single starting group that spreads out over time, three to eight groups per stage and 20 to 99 bands per diagram, while every count behind the picture is known exactly. The flow table records the size of every group at every stage and the number of people in every band between two stages. An automatic check confirms that the bands leaving and entering every group add up to the size of the group.

### Drawing the maps

The maps are drawn with Matplotlib [@hunter2007] in the layout of the XR-CareerAssist career maps (Figure 2). Four columns stand for now, 2 years, 5 years and 10 years. Each group is a coloured bar whose height is proportional to its size, with a minimum height so that small groups stay visible, and each band is a curved ribbon whose width is proportional to the number of people it carries. The name of each group is printed beside its bar, followed by its size in brackets, and a title above the diagram describes the questionnaire and gives the total number of profiles.

The default drawing is 1,024 pixels wide on a white background. Eleven other styles each change one setting of the default, namely a transparent background, the problem met with BLIP in the project [@tantaroudas2026ore], names without numbers, numbers also printed on every band, text 30% smaller or 40% larger, shades of one colour instead of distinct colours, widths of 512, 768 or 1,536 pixels, groups in a fixed order instead of by size, and no title.

### Questions with exact answers

Eleven question templates cover what a user might ask an assistant about a career map (Table 1). Some ask for a name, such as the largest group at a stage or the main destination of the people who leave a group. Others ask for a number, such as the size of a group, its share of all profiles or the number of people in a band, and others ask for yes or no, or whether a group grows or shrinks. One template asks the reader to follow, for two or three stages, the largest band that leads to a different group, which needs several steps of reasoning. Templates are skipped when their answer would be ambiguous, for example when two groups have the same size.

Every answer is computed from the flow table, and a second, independent routine recomputes it before the question is stored. Each question also records what a reader must see in the picture to answer it exactly. Most questions need only the names of the groups and a comparison of sizes, but three need numbers, the size of a group (printed beside its name), the size of a band (printed only in the style with numbers on the bands) and the total (printed in the title).

### The benchmark

The benchmark contains 600 cohorts, 358 role maps and 242 industry maps, with about 11 questions each, 6,560 questions in total. The first 400 cohorts form the training split and the last 200 the test split, so no test diagram is ever used for training. Sixty test cohorts are also drawn in all twelve styles, which gives 1,260 images in total. The main evaluation uses the first 80 test cohorts in the default style, 875 questions, and the style experiment uses the first 20 of the cohorts drawn in every style, 220 questions per style.

### Readers

Five models and two baselines were compared (Table 2). The models were chosen because they are openly available and run on a laptop processor, as a headset application would need.

- SmolVLM-256M and SmolVLM-500M, general vision-language models designed to be small [@marafioti2025], each given the image as one view whose longer side is 512 pixels.
- MatCha-ChartQA, a model pre-trained to read charts and fine-tuned on the ChartQA benchmark of bar, line and pie charts [@liu2023matcha, @masry2022, @lee2023pix2struct].
- BLIP-VQA, the base BLIP model for visual question answering [@li2022blip], the model family used in XR-CareerAssist, without further training.
- Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, a small language model [@qwen2025] that never sees the picture and receives the flow table as plain text, for example "Stage 5 years, Manager 36, Team Lead 89", in the way that language models are asked to reason over tables [@chen2023table].
- An exact query on the flow table, the answer that an application obtains when it looks the value up in its own data instead of asking a model. It confirms that every question can be answered from the table.

Two baselines show what can be achieved without reading anything. The first is a random choice among the possible answers. The second is a guess that never looks at the map. It names the starting role or industry for name questions, gives the most common answer of the training split for yes or no and for rise or fall questions, and the median answer of the training split for number questions.

All models were downloaded from the Hugging Face hub and run with the Transformers library [@wolf2020] in 32-bit precision with greedy decoding, so that runs can be repeated exactly. The picture readers and the table reader received the question after a short instruction asking for a short answer, a name, a number, yes or no, or increase, decrease or stay the same. The exact versions of all models are listed with the data [@sankeyqadata].

### Scoring

Each reply is parsed into an answer. For name questions the first possible name that occurs in the reply is taken, and for yes or no and for rise or fall questions the first matching word. A number counts as correct when it is within 5% of the exact value, or within one unit for small values, which is the relaxed accuracy used by chart question answering benchmarks [@methani2020, @masry2022]. Exact matches are also reported. Replies that contain no usable answer count as wrong. Uncertainty is shown as 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of cohorts [@efron1979], because all questions about one diagram share the same picture. Differences between readers or between styles are computed on the same questions and resampled in the same way.

### Fine-tuning with few or many diagrams

To repeat the observation of the XR-CareerAssist project under controlled conditions, BLIP-VQA was fine-tuned on training diagrams chosen in two ways. In the diverse selection, the image features of all 400 training diagrams were grouped into 20 clusters with k-means [@lloyd1982, @pedregosa2011], and the diagram nearest to the centre of each cluster was chosen, an idea also used to pick representative examples in active learning [@sener2018]. In the random selection, 20, 100 or all 400 diagrams were drawn at random, the 20-diagram draw with three different seeds. All questions of the chosen diagrams were used for training.

Every run made the same number of updates, 600 steps of 8 questions, with the AdamW optimiser [@loshchilov2019] at a learning rate of 0.00003, so the runs differ only in which diagrams they saw. A run on 20 diagrams therefore passed over its data about 22 times and a run on 400 diagrams about once. To make training possible on a processor, the part of BLIP that encodes the image was kept fixed and its output computed once per diagram, and only the parts that read the question and write the answer were trained. Each run took about 45 minutes. Each fine-tuned model was evaluated on the 875 questions of the main evaluation, and the model trained on 400 diagrams was also tested on the drawing styles, together with SmolVLM-256M and BLIP-VQA before fine-tuning. Images with a transparent background were placed on black before they were given to a model, which is what a reader that ignores transparency sees.

## Results

### Small models rarely beat a guess

Table 3 and Figure 3 show the main results. The exact query on the table answered all 875 questions correctly, which confirms that every question has one right answer that the data contain. None of the models came close. The best picture reader, SmolVLM-500M, answered 30.1% of the questions correctly and its smaller version 27.7%. The chart model MatCha-ChartQA answered 16.0% and BLIP-VQA 12.7%, below the 17.9% expected from choosing at random among the possible answers. The small language model reading the table answered 38.9%. A guess that never looks at the map reached 40.5%, as much as the table reader and more than every picture reader.

The paired differences in Table 4 make the same point. Compared with the table reader, SmolVLM-500M answered 8.8 percentage points fewer questions correctly, SmolVLM-256M 11.2 fewer, MatCha-ChartQA 22.9 fewer and BLIP-VQA 26.2 fewer, and none of these intervals includes zero. The guess was 1.6 points ahead of the table reader, a difference within the uncertainty. MatCha-ChartQA and BLIP-VQA often gave replies that were not answers at all, for example a position such as "second from left", which was the case for 31.8% and 42.7% of their replies. All models answered in one to three seconds per question on two processor cores, so speed did not decide between them (Table 3 and Figure 4).

### Which questions fail

Table 5 breaks the results down by kind of question. Questions that need the numbers printed in the map were the hardest for the picture readers. For the total number of profiles, printed in the title, no SmolVLM answer was correct, while the table reader was always right. For the size of a group, printed beside its name, the picture readers were correct in 5.0% to 16.2% of cases. The SmolVLM models mostly replied with small numbers such as 1, 2 or 10, which suggests that at 512 pixels the printed digits are too small for them to read.

Questions that only need a comparison of sizes went better. SmolVLM-256M named the largest group at a stage correctly in 58.8% of cases and chose the larger of two flows in 53.8%, close to the 50.0% of a coin toss. Following the main moves over several stages was rarely right for any model, at most 28.0%. Whether a group rises or falls was answered at about the rate of the guess by SmolVLM-256M, while the table reader answered "stay the same" to every such question and was almost never right. The table reader also miscounted the groups at a stage and could not compute shares. It did best on lookups that appear literally in the table, such as the total (100.0%) and the larger of two flows (78.8%).

The guess did well on questions whose answers follow the structure of the maps. Most cohorts keep the starting role as one of the largest groups, so naming it was often right, and most stages show about six groups. This shows that a benchmark of this kind must report such a baseline, because a model can look competent by learning the typical answer without reading the picture. Results were similar for role maps and industry maps, although the picture readers did somewhat better on industry maps (Table 6).

### Fine-tuning helps but does not solve the task

Table 8 and Figure 6 show the fine-tuning experiment. After fine-tuning on 20 diagrams, BLIP-VQA answered 43.9% to 45.7% of the test questions correctly, up from 12.7% before fine-tuning, and above the guess. The 20 diverse diagrams chosen by k-means gave 45.3%, within the range of the three random draws of 20 diagrams. Diversity changed which questions the model learned rather than how many. The diverse selection did better on name questions (51.1% against 39.0% to 43.5%) and worse on number questions (33.5% against 39.0% to 42.2%).

More diagrams helped more than a careful choice of diagrams. With the same number of training steps, 100 random diagrams gave 54.2% and all 400 training diagrams 55.5%. The fine-tuned models learned to count the groups at a stage and to judge rises and falls, but they still read printed numbers poorly, and even the best run answered fewer than six in ten questions correctly. The training loss of the runs on 20 diagrams fell close to zero, which is a sign that the model memorised its few training diagrams.

## Discussion

### Read the data, show the picture

The central result is simple. When an application has the table that a chart was drawn from, it should answer questions from the table and not from the picture. A direct look-up was always right and took less than a millisecond, while every model, including a language model given the same table, was wrong most of the time. The picture remains valuable for the user, who sees the shape of the possible careers at a glance, but the explanation should come from the data. This confirms, with measurements, the design decision taken in XR-CareerAssist, where the conversational agent rather than the vision-language model guided users through the maps [@tantaroudas2026ore, @tantaroudas2026arxiv]. In practice the assistant can pass the user's question to a small set of look-up functions, the same functions that produce the exact answers in this benchmark, and let the language model only phrase the answer.

The weakness of the table reader was not expected. A 0.5-billion-parameter language model could copy values that appear in the table, such as the total, but it could not count, add or follow flows, and it fell into repeating one option of the instruction. Larger language models reason over tables much better [@chen2023table], but they need more memory and time than a headset application may have. Exact look-ups need neither.

### Why picture readers fail on Sankey diagrams

The picture readers had three difficulties. First, they could not read small printed numbers at the resolution they use, so questions about sizes and totals were mostly wrong. Second, Sankey diagrams encode quantities in the widths of curved bands, which are hard to compare even for people [@cleveland1984], and the bands of a career map cross and merge [@zarate2018]. Third, the chart model MatCha-ChartQA was trained on bar, line and pie charts [@masry2022, @liu2023matcha], and its knowledge did not transfer to flow diagrams. Its replies were often not answers, which matches reports that vision-language models produce plausible but ungrounded text [@li2023hallucination]. Earlier chart benchmarks [@kahou2018, @kafle2018, @methani2020, @masry2022] did not include Sankey diagrams, so models have had little chance to learn them.

### What fine-tuning can and cannot do

Fine-tuning raised the accuracy of BLIP-VQA more than fourfold, so the task is learnable, but the best run still answered fewer than six in ten questions correctly. The experiment does not support the idea that a small set of diverse diagrams is better than a random one. In the project, the 1,280 training diagrams were near copies of each other [@tantaroudas2026ore], so choosing different ones removed duplicates. In SankeyQA every diagram comes from a different cohort, so a random draw is already diverse, and k-means only shifted the model's strengths from number questions to name questions. The lesson for small projects is that variety in the training data matters, and that more varied diagrams help more than a clever choice among few.

### Limitations

The cohorts are artificial and were generated only to demonstrate and test the technology. They have the structure of real career maps, but real maps may contain more groups, longer names and patterns that the simulation does not produce, and a model fine-tuned on SankeyQA should be checked on real maps before use. The tested models are small, chosen to run on a processor, and larger vision-language models will do better. Their results can be added with the same code. Each model received one instruction, and different prompts may change the results. The scorer reads the first possible answer in a reply, which favours short replies, and the relaxed rule for numbers accepts answers within one unit, which helps guesses of small numbers. The evaluation sets are moderate in size, which the confidence intervals show. Finally, only BLIP was fine-tuned, and only part of it.

### Future work

The benchmark can grow in three directions. Larger and newer vision-language models can be evaluated with the same questions, real career maps can be added once their data can be shared in aggregate form, and the assistant of XR-CareerAssist can be changed to answer through look-up functions and tested with users.

## Conclusions

SankeyQA is an open benchmark of career-map Sankey diagrams with questions whose answers are known exactly. On it, small vision-language models that run on a laptop answered fewer than one in three questions correctly, no better than a guess that ignores the picture, and a small language model reading the table did little better. Fine-tuning one picture model on 400 diagrams raised its accuracy to 55.5%, and twenty diverse diagrams were no better than twenty random ones. An assistant that explains career maps, in virtual reality or elsewhere, should therefore answer from the data behind the map and use the map to show the answer. The benchmark, its generator, all model replies and the code that produced every table and figure are openly available for testing future models.
